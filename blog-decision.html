The product decision
before the feature.

Why a clear problem statement often does more work than an impressive solution list.

Sample articleProduct thinking
On this pageThe problemUseful questionsIn practice

A feature request usually arrives with a shape already attached to it. Build a dashboard. Add a filter. Redesign the flow. The shape can be useful, but it is not the same thing as understanding the problem.

Start with the problem

A good product decision begins by making the situation concrete. Who is struggling? What are they trying to do? What evidence tells us the current experience is not working?

The feature is a possible answer. The problem is the thing we are responsible for understanding.

Questions that create signal

Before a team commits to a solution, I like to make a few questions explicit:

  • What user behaviour or outcome are we trying to change?
  • What evidence do we have, and what are we assuming?
  • What would be the smallest useful change we could test?
  • How will we know whether the situation improved?

Make the decision visible

Writing down the problem, desired outcome, constraints, and decision owner gives a team something to work from. It also makes it easier to revisit the decision when new evidence appears.

A practical starting note

Try a short brief with four lines: the user situation, the business context, the outcome, and the next test. It is enough structure to create alignment without pretending the answer is finished.
